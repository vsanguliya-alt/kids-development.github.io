1 Year Old Separation Anxiety: 7 Signs Parents Should Know

Separation anxiety is a normal and healthy developmental stage for a 1-year-old. It typically begins around 8 months, peaks between 10 to 18 months, and usually resolves by 24 months of age . During this phase, children become anxious when separated from their primary caregivers because they have not yet fully developed "object permanence"—the understanding that something still exists even when out of sight . While this behavior is expected, knowing the specific signs helps parents distinguish between a normal developmental phase and a potential issue.

7 Signs of Separation Anxiety

1. Crying and Clinging

This is the most common sign. A 1-year-old may cry, scream, or physically cling to a parent when they attempt to leave the room or hand the child to another caregiver .

2. Tantrums at Separation

Some children may throw a tantrum, whine, or beg their parent not to leave . These scenes can be dramatic and feel painful for both the child and the parent .

3. Rejecting Other Caregivers

A toddler might actively reject another caregiver (such as at daycare) in an attempt to make the parent stay . If this refusal is accompanied by other signs of tension, such as trouble sleeping or loss of appetite, it could indicate a problem with the childcare situation .

4. Difficulty with Bedtime and Sleep

Fear of separation can manifest as resistance to going to bed or frequent nighttime awakenings if a parent is not present . While this is common, it is a key sign of separation-related distress.

5. Excessive Worry

Children with significant separation anxiety may express excessive worry about being lost, or about their parent being harmed (e.g., in a car accident) .

6. Physical Symptoms (Somatic Complaints)

When faced with separation, some children may develop physical symptoms such as a stomachache, headache, or nausea . These are real physical manifestations of anxiety.

7. Panic Symptoms

In more intense cases, a child may show signs of a panic attack, such as nausea, vomiting, or shortness of breath, before a parent leaves .

When to Worry: Normal vs. A Concern

For a 1-year-old, these signs are normal as long as they are resolved by the time the child is 24 months old . Typically, this anxiety does not cause worry and does not require a doctor's evaluation .

However, parents should be concerned if the separation anxiety:

Persists beyond age 2 years .

Interferes significantly with daily life, such as preventing the child from attending daycare, preschool, or playing normally with peers .

Lasts for more than 4 weeks and includes symptoms like nightmares about separation or excessive, persistent worry .

These signs could indicate separation anxiety disorder, a more serious condition that requires professional evaluation .

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor about my 1-year-old's separation anxiety?

Call your pediatrician if your child's anxiety persists beyond 24 months, interferes with their ability to attend daycare or play with peers, or if they have severe panic symptoms or nightmares about separation .

Q: How long does separation anxiety normally last?

Separation anxiety typically begins around 8 months, peaks between 10 and 18 months, and generally resolves by the time a child is 24 months old .

Q: What home remedies help with separation anxiety?

Establish brief, calm goodbye routines, distract your child with a toy or activity as you leave, and ensure the temporary caregiver is someone familiar to the child. Do not return immediately when your child cries from another room; instead, call out to reassure them .

Q: Is it normal for a 1-year-old to cry when I leave the room?

Yes, this is a very normal and positive sign of a healthy attachment. It shows your child has bonded with you and feels secure with you as their caregiver .

Q: Does separation anxiety affect a child's development?

Normal separation anxiety causes no long-term harm if it resolves by age 2. If it persists, it may interfere with a child's ability to engage in age-appropriate activities like attending preschool or playing with peers .

Q: When should I go to the ER for a child with anxiety?

Go to the ER if your child experiences a severe panic attack with symptoms like shortness of breath or vomiting, or if they express thoughts of self-harm .
