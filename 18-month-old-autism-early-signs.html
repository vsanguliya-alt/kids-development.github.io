Autism Early Signs in 18-Month-Olds: A Parent&#x27;s Guide

Autism spectrum disorder (ASD) is a neurological and developmental disorder that begins early in childhood and lasts throughout a person&#x27;s life . Most parents of children with ASD suspect that something is wrong by the time the child is 18 months old . Recognizing the early signs is the first step toward getting your child the support they may need.

Key Red Flags to Watch For

The American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) recommends that all children be screened specifically for autism at their 18- and 24-month well-child visits . The following &quot;red flags&quot; may indicate a child is at risk for ASD and needs an evaluation :

Does not point to show you something interesting – by 18 months, children typically point to share interest in objects or events .

Does not respond to their name – consistently failing to turn when called can be an early warning sign .

Does not engage in pretend play – for example, not pretending to feed a doll or stuffed animal .

Does not use gestures – such as pointing, waving &quot;bye-bye,&quot; or shaking head &quot;no&quot; .

Language delays – not saying single words, or losing words they once had (developmental regression) .

Poor eye contact – avoiding eye contact or not looking when smiled at .

Repetitive movements – such as hand flapping, rocking, or spinning .

Intense or violent tantrums – especially over minor changes in routine .

Tuning people out – appearing to be &quot;in their own world&quot; .

Other Social and Communication Signs

Children with ASD often have problems with social interactions and verbal and nonverbal communication . Additional signs to look for include:

Does not adjust gaze to look at objects that others are looking at .

Is not interested in other children .

Has unusual reactions to sensory input, such as loud sounds, certain textures, or crowds .

Gets very upset when routines are changed .

Repeats words or phrases over and over (echolalia) .

What to Do If You Have Concerns

Early identification of developmental disorders is critical to the well-being of children and their families . Acting early can lead to better outcomes for your child .

Talk to your pediatrician – share your observations and ask for a formal developmental and autism screening .

Trust your instincts – you know your child best. If the first doctor doesn&#x27;t take your concerns seriously, get a second opinion .

Do not wait – children should be referred for intervention for all identified developmental delays at the time of identification and not wait for an autism diagnostic evaluation to take place .
