Separation Anxiety in Your 3-Year-Old: A Guide for Parents

Separation anxiety is a common and normal part of your child&#x27;s development. At 3 years old, your child is becoming more independent but may still experience distress when separated from you. This can be a time of significant emotional growth, as they learn to navigate the world both with and without you. Child development includes physical, intellectual, social, and emotional changes (Source: NIH).

Why Separation Anxiety Happens

Separation anxiety is a sign of healthy attachment. At 3 years old, your child:

Has a strong bond with you and sees you as their safe base.

Is developing a sense of self and may feel vulnerable without you.

Lacks the concept of time, so they may worry that when you leave, you won&#x27;t return.

Is also asserting their independence, which can make them feel anxious.

Signs of Separation Anxiety

Your 3-year-old may show separation anxiety through:

Crying or clinging when you try to leave.

Refusing to go to preschool or other caregivers.

Waking up at night and calling for you.

Being clingy and wanting to be held frequently.

Showing distress during drop-offs at school or with other caregivers.

Complaining of stomachaches or headaches before separations.

How to Help Your Child Cope

You can ease your child&#x27;s anxiety with these strategies:

Practice short separations and gradually increase the time you are away.

Establish a consistent goodbye routine, such as a special hug or wave.

Say goodbye with a smile and leave without lingering.

Reassure your child that you will return and always follow through.

Encourage positive interactions with teachers and other caregivers.

Read books about separation, such as &quot;The Kissing Hand&quot; or &quot;Llama Llama Misses Mama.&quot;

Provide a comfort object, like a stuffed animal or blanket.

Validate their feelings and help them understand their emotions.

When to Seek Professional Help

Separation anxiety is usually a normal phase, but if it interferes with your child&#x27;s daily functioning or lasts beyond toddlerhood, talk to your pediatrician. They can offer guidance and support. If your child shows signs of a more serious problem, such as problems in more than one setting, changes in appetite or sleep, or signs of being upset, such as sadness or tearfulness, it may be a sign of a more serious issue. In these cases, a doctor or mental health specialist can provide a comprehensive evaluation.
