Speech Delay in Toddlers: When to Worry

Watching your toddler learn to talk is an exciting milestone, but it can also be a source of worry when their progress seems slower than other children&#x27;s. While every child develops at their own pace, knowing the key milestones and red flags can help you understand when to seek professional advice.

Understanding the Milestones: What&#x27;s Expected by Age 2

Most children reach certain speech and language milestones by age 2, which serve as helpful guideposts. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), by the end of their second year, most toddlers can:

Say at least two words together, like &quot;more milk&quot; or &quot;Daddy go&quot;

Point to things in a book when you ask, &quot;Where is the bear?&quot;

Point to at least two body parts when asked

Use more gestures than just waving and pointing, such as blowing a kiss or nodding yes

🚩 When to Worry: Key Red Flags for Speech Delay

Not meeting milestones can be a sign of a delay. Health authorities like HealthLink BC and ColumbiaDoctors list several specific &quot;red flags&quot; for a speech or language delay. The most critical red flags for a 2-year-old include:

Not combining two words by 24 months

Understanding and speaking fewer than 50 words

Speaking unclearly: Having problems understanding your child&#x27;s speech at 24 months, or strangers being unable to understand them by 36 months

Not showing an interest in communicating

A sudden loss of speech or language skills at any age

Sometimes a delay may be caused by hearing loss. Other times it may be due to a speech or language disorder (Source: NIH | Speech and Language Problems in Children).

Taking Action: What to Do If You&#x27;re Concerned

If your child is not meeting one or more milestones, has lost skills, or you have other concerns, experts consistently advise to &quot;act early.&quot;

Talk to Your Pediatrician: Share your concerns with your child&#x27;s doctor. They can evaluate your child, rule out medical issues like hearing loss, and perform or refer you for a developmental screening.

Ask for a Referral: If concerns persist after screening, ask for a referral to a specialist, such as a speech-language pathologist, for a more in-depth diagnostic evaluation.

Contact Early Intervention: Reach out to your state or territory&#x27;s early intervention program, which can connect your child with services that may help. Early identification and intervention are key because they can prevent speech delays from interfering with school learning and psychosocial adjustment.
