3-Year-Old Milestones: A Guide for Parents

Mastering new skills such as how to walk, talk, and use the potty are developmental milestones (Source: NIH). The third year of life is a period of remarkable growth and change. By age 3, your child is becoming more independent, communicative, and mobile. Remember that toddlers do not develop at the same rate (Source: NIH), so your child may reach some milestones earlier or later than others.

Physical Milestones

At 3 years old, most children can:

Run with more speed and agility.

Climb confidently on playground equipment.

Jump off low steps and jump in place.

Pedal a tricycle.

Walk backwards and balance on one foot for a few seconds.

Kick and throw a ball with more coordination.

Feed themselves with a spoon and fork.

Draw a circle and other simple shapes.

Cognitive Milestones

At this age, your child is developing important thinking skills. They can:

Follow two- or three-step directions.

Sort objects by shape and color.

Engage in more complex pretend play, such as playing "house" or "doctor."

Ask questions like "why?" and "what's that?"

Remember and recall familiar routines and events.

Begin to understand the concept of time.

Language Milestones

Language development takes off during the preschool years. By age 3, expect your child to:

Use sentences of 3 to 5 words and speak clearly.

Understand and follow three-step directions.

Ask questions like "why?" and "what's that?"

Have a vocabulary of several hundred words.

Use pronouns and plurals correctly.

Social and Emotional Milestones

Your 3-year-old is becoming more social and aware of their own emotions. They may:

Show empathy, such as offering comfort to a crying friend.

Engage in cooperative play with other children.

Develop friendships and show a preference for certain playmates.

Become more independent, yet still seek comfort and security from you.

Express a wide range of emotions and begin to manage them.

When to Talk to Your Pediatrician

If you have concerns about your child's development, don't hesitate to talk to your pediatrician. Developmental and behavioral screening tests are available, and your doctor can monitor your child's progress and address any delays early on. Early intervention can make a significant difference.
