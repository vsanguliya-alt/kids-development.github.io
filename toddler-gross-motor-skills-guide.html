Toddler Gross Motor Skills in Children 0: Complete Parent Guide

Gross motor skills involve the large muscles of the body and are essential for movements like rolling, sitting, crawling, walking, running, and climbing . These foundational skills are not just about physical fitness—they are closely linked to brain development, self-regulation, and school readiness. Understanding typical milestones and knowing when to seek help can support your child's development.

What Are Gross Motor Skills?

Gross motor skills are the abilities required to control the large muscles of the body. They are used for activities like sitting, standing, walking, running, and maintaining balance. Gross motor development is one of the key areas of a child's development, along with fine motor, sensory, language, and social skills (Source: NIH | Toddler Development: MedlinePlus – Toddler Development). Gross motor milestones are a key indicator of overall neurological development.

Typical Gross Motor Milestones by Age

0Months: Early Motor Control

0Months: Newborns gradually gain head control, turning their head side to side while on their stomach and pushing up on their arms.

3Months: Rolls over from tummy to back (around 4 months) and back to tummy (around 5months). Begins to sit with support.

6Months: Mobility Begins

6Months: Sits without support; begins to crawl or scoot. Many babies start pulling to stand.

9Months: Pulls to stand and cruises along furniture. May take first steps (most by 12months).

12Months: First Steps

Walks independently (usually by 18 months). Climbs onto furniture, runs stiffly, and begins to climb stairs.

18Months: Becoming More Confident

Runs with more coordination. Walks up and down stairs with support. Kicks a ball.

2Years: Jumping and Running

Runs and climbs well. Jumps in place. Rides a tricycle. Walks up and down stairs independently. Stands on one foot briefly.

3Years: Advanced Motor Skills

3 Years: Walks up and down stairs with alternating feet. Runs, climbs, and jumps.

4 Years: Hops on one foot; throws ball overhand; uses playground equipment.

5 Years: Skips, catches a ball, and balances on one foot for longer periods.

Red Flags: When to Be Concerned

You should discuss your child's development with your pediatrician if they:

Are not rolling over by 6 months.

Cannot sit without support by 9 months.

Are not crawling or moving around by 12 months.

Are not walking by 18 months.

Walk only on their toes consistently.

Are not running or climbing by 2 years.

Show a loss of previously acquired skills (regression).

Have difficulty balancing or appear clumsy compared to peers.

How to Support Gross Motor Development

Give your child plenty of floor time: Provide safe space for crawling, rolling, and exploring.

Encourage outdoor play: Running, climbing, jumping, and playing with balls.

Offer safe climbing opportunities: Playgrounds, soft play areas, and indoor climbing toys.

Limit screen time: Avoid prolonged periods in containers like swings or bouncers.

Celebrate progress: Praise efforts and milestones to build confidence.

The Bottom Line

Gross motor skills are an essential part of a child's development. While there is a wide range of normal, monitoring your child's progress and knowing the red flags can help you ensure they are on track. If you have any concerns, talk to your pediatrician.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor about my child's gross motor skills?

Call your pediatrician if your child is not meeting expected milestones for their age, such as rolling over, sitting, or walking. Also call if you notice a loss of previously acquired skills.

Q: How can I help my child develop gross motor skills?

Provide plenty of floor time, encourage outdoor active play, offer safe climbing opportunities, limit screen time, and celebrate your child's progress.

Q: What activities are best for gross motor development?

Activities include tummy time, crawling games, walking practice, running, climbing, jumping, hopping, skipping, and throwing and catching balls.

Q: When should I go to the ER for a motor skill issue?

Go to the ER immediately if your child has a sudden loss of motor skills, especially if accompanied by other symptoms like a fever, severe headache, difficulty breathing, or a seizure.
