1-Year-Old Fine Motor Skills: What to Expect

Fine motor skills are the small movements of the hands, fingers, and wrists that are essential for everyday tasks. At 1 year old, your child is beginning to develop the dexterity they will use for the rest of their life. The normal development of children aged 1includes areas such as fine motor skills, which involve activities like feeding themselves and drawing (Source: NIH).

Fine Motor Milestones at 12 Months

At one year old, you can expect your child to be developing the ability to:

Use the pincer grasp, which means picking up small objects like a piece of cereal between their thumb and forefinger (Source: NIH).

Hold a spoon, though they may not be able to use it effectively yet (Source: NIH).

Transfer objects from one hand to the other.

Point at objects they want or are interested in.

Drop and pick up toys intentionally as they explore cause and effect.

Fine Motor Milestones at 18 Months

By 18 months, your child's fine motor skills become more refined. You may notice them:

Feeding themselves more successfully with a spoon and their fingers (Source: NIH).

Drawing and making marks on paper with a crayon (Source: NIH).

Stacking blocks and manipulating small toys.

Turning pages in a board book.

Opening and closing cabinets and drawers.

How to Support Fine Motor Development

You can encourage your child's fine motor skills through everyday play and activities:

Provide toys that promote grasping, such as blocks, stacking rings, and shape sorters.

Offer finger foods like small pieces of soft fruit or cereal.

Encourage drawing with chunky, easy-to-grip crayons.

Practice self-feeding with a spoon and fork.

Allow time for free play, which is essential for motor skill development.

When to Talk to Your Pediatrician

Toddlers do not develop at the same rate (Source: NIH). However, if you have concerns about your child's motor skills, or if they seem to be lagging behind in their development, talk to your pediatrician. Developmental screening involves giving your child a short test to check for motor, movement, or other developmental delays (Source: NIH). Your provider will check your child's motor skills, muscle tone, reflexes, and posture (Source: NIH). Early intervention can make a significant difference.
