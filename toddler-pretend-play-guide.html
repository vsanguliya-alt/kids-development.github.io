Toddler Pretend Play in Children 0: Complete Parent Guide

Pretend play is a magical milestone in your toddler's development. When your child picks up a block and pretends it's a phone, or feeds a teddy bear with an empty spoon, they are doing more than just having fun—they are building essential life skills that support language, social understanding, and future mental health.

What Is Pretend Play?

Pretend play (also called symbolic play or imaginative play) is when a child uses their imagination to give new meaning to objects or actions. Your toddler might pretend a doll is a real baby, use a block as a telephone, or act out familiar routines like cooking or going to the shop .

Pretend play and language development are closely linked because both involve symbols. In pretend play, your child uses a spoon as a "symbol" for a brush. In language, words are symbols to express thoughts and ideas. That's why pretend play typically emerges around the same time as first words—between 12 and 18 months .

Why Pretend Play Matters

Language and Communication Skills

Pretend play builds vocabulary, supports expressive and receptive language, and helps children practice using words to describe actions and negotiate roles. Children who engage in pretend play often demonstrate stronger language abilities .

Social and Emotional Development

When toddlers pretend, they practice taking on different roles, which helps them see the world from others' perspectives. This builds empathy, self-regulation, and social skills . Playing with others teaches turn-taking, negotiation, and cooperation.

Imagination, Creativity, and Problem-Solving

Pretend play kindles imagination and encourages children to come up with new ideas. Since there is no one "right way" to pretend, toddlers learn to be resourceful and solve problems creatively .

Long-Term Mental Health Benefits

A 2026 Australian study of more than 1,400 children found that those with stronger pretend play skills at ages two and three were less likely to experience emotional and behavioural difficulties in the early primary years—even after accounting for socioeconomic position, maternal mental health, and language ability . The researchers noted that pretend play may support embodied cognition, where movement, imagination, and brain development interact in ways still being explored .

Typical Stages of Pretend Play

Pretend play develops in predictable stages, though every child progresses at their own pace.

Stage 1: Self Pretend Play (12–18 months)

Your toddler performs one pretend action on themselves—pretending to eat, drink, or sleep. They tend to use realistic-looking toys .

Stage 2: Simple Pretend Play (18–24 months)

Your toddler starts to carry out pretend actions on toys or people. They might give a doll a drink, make a car "drive," or imitate familiar actions like talking on a toy phone .

Stage 3: Familiar Routines (24–30 months)

Toddlers act out sequences of pretend actions related to familiar routines—putting a doll to bed, cooking a meal. They may start using less realistic objects, such as a toy banana as a phone .

Stage 4: Expanding Imagination (30–36 months)

Your toddler explores less familiar themes and routines, like pretending to go to the doctor. They might pretend without an object at all, using their hand as a cup, or use an object that doesn't resemble the real thing .

Stage 5: Imaginative Role Play with Others (3–5 years)

Play becomes more complex and social. Children act out imaginary themes—fairies, superheroes, dinosaurs—and take on different roles with other children. Language often drives the play at this stage, with children explaining their roles and using words to act them out .

How to Encourage Pretend Play

Model pretend play. Show your toddler how to "feed" a doll or "talk" on a toy phone. Narrate what you're doing: "I'm stirring the soup in the pot" .

Follow your child's lead. Let your toddler direct the play. Use open-ended comments rather than instructions, and join in low-power roles when invited .

Provide simple props. A play kitchen, dress-up clothes, or everyday items like pots, pans, and plastic plates are excellent for pretend play . You don't need expensive toys—real-life objects work just as well.

Read stories together. Toddlers often recreate stories they've heard, building their imaginative worlds from what they've read .

Allow uninterrupted play time. Research suggests protecting imaginative play time may be one of the simplest investments in children's long-term wellbeing .

Red Flags: When to Seek Help

While pretend play is normal and beneficial, some signs warrant a discussion with your pediatrician.

When to call the doctor:

Your toddler is not engaging in any pretend play by 18–24 months, especially alongside speech delays or social difficulties Your child is not talking much or at all by age 2, not making eye contact, or not responding to their name They show no interest in others or don't use simple gestures like pointing or waving The intensity of pretend play disrupts eating, sleeping, dressing, or socializing Your child seems "stuck" in a troubling pretend scenario, especially replaying a trauma or fixating on harm There is a loss of previously acquired skills at any age

If you notice any of these signs, talk to your child's health care provider. They can assess your child's development, rule out hearing or medical issues, and guide you to appropriate specialists if needed .

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor about my toddler's pretend play?

Call your pediatrician if your toddler shows no pretend play by 18–24 months, especially alongside speech delays or social difficulties. Also seek advice if pretend play intensity disrupts daily activities, if your child regresses in skills, or if you have any concerns about development .

Q: Is it normal for my toddler to pretend to be an animal all the time?

Yes, this is typically normal. Intense pretend play is a common part of toddler and preschool development and often helps children practice perspective-taking and process emotions. It's only a concern if it disrupts eating, sleeping, or socializing .

Q: What home activities encourage pretend play?

Read stories, model pretend actions like feeding a doll, provide simple props like pots and pans, let your child take the lead, and allow uninterrupted imaginative play time .

Q: Does pretend play affect language development?

Yes, pretend play and language development are closely linked. Both involve using symbols—in play, a spoon becomes a brush; in language, words represent ideas. Children who engage in pretend play often develop stronger language skills .

Q: When should I go to the ER for a developmental concern?

Go to the ER immediately if your child has a sudden loss of skills, confusion, or difficulty swallowing or breathing. For non-emergency developmental concerns, contact your pediatrician.
