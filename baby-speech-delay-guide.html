Baby Milestones in Children 0: Complete Parent Guide

Watching your child grow and learn new skills is an incredible journey. Milestones are the key skills that most children reach by a certain age—like taking a first step, smiling, and waving "bye-bye" .

This guide provides an overview of developmental milestones from birth through age 5, based on trusted sources from pediatric organizations, to help you understand what to expect and when to seek support.

What Are Developmental Milestones?

Developmental milestones are the skills that most children can do by a specific age. They are typically grouped into categories: physical (gross and fine motor), language, cognitive (thinking and learning), and social/emotional development .

A critical note: There is a wide range of what is considered "normal" in child development . Your child may be ahead in some areas and slightly behind in others, and that's okay. The order in which skills develop is generally consistent, but the timing can vary significantly .

Milestones: Birth to 12 Months

The first year is a time of rapid transformation, from a newborn's reflexes to a curious, mobile infant.

0Months:

Physical: Lifts head slightly when on tummy; has reflexes like grasping your finger and startling at loud noises .

Social/Language: Cries to communicate needs; begins to coo and make vowel sounds ("ah," "oo") .

2Months:

Physical: Holds head up with more control; can track moving objects with eyes; begins to swipe at dangling toys .

Social/Language: Smiles spontaneously at people; coos and gurgles; turns head toward sounds .

4Months:

Physical: Rolls over from tummy to back and back to tummy; can sit with support and may sit briefly without it; begins to reach and grasp objects .

Social/Language: Babbles, making sounds like "ba-ba" or "ma-ma"; laughs; recognizes familiar faces .

6Months:

Physical: Sits without support; may begin to crawl or scoot; can transfer objects from one hand to the other .

Social/Language: Responds to own name; understands "no"; may show separation anxiety .

9Months:

Physical: Pulls to stand; cruises along furniture; may take first steps .

Social/Language: Uses gestures like pointing or waving "bye-bye"; says simple words like "mama" and "dada" .

Milestones: 1 to 3 Years (Toddlerhood)

The toddler years are marked by a burst in independence, physical ability, and language development .

12Months:

Physical: Walks independently; can scribble; can use a spoon with some spills .

Social/Language: Says a few words beyond "mama/dada"; follows simple one-step commands .

18Months:

Physical: Walks up steps with help; builds a tower of several blocks; runs and climbs .

Social/Language: Uses 2word phrases ("more milk"); points to body parts; vocabulary expands rapidly .

24Months:

Physical: Jumps in place; turns pages of a book one at a time; can draw a circle .

Social/Language: Uses 2word sentences; strangers can understand about half of what they say .

30Months:

Physical: Rides a tricycle; stands on one foot briefly; dresses self with help .

Social/Language: Speaks in short sentences; knows own name, age, and sex .

Milestones: 3 to 5 Years (Preschool)

Preschoolers become increasingly social, imaginative, and physically coordinated .

3 Years:

Physical: Runs with coordination; climbs; walks up and down stairs .

Social/Language: Asks "why" and "what" questions; speaks clearly in sentences; knows 3colors .

4 Years:

Physical: Hops on one foot; throws ball overhand; uses scissors; dresses and undresses .

Social/Language: Counts to 4; knows 4 colors; uses pronouns and prepositions .

5 Years:

Physical: Skips and catches a bounced ball; draws recognizable shapes like triangles; uses a fork and knife .

Social/Language: Counts to 10; speaks in complete sentences; tells a simple story; knows address or phone number .

Red Flags: When to Seek Help

If your child misses certain milestones, it can be a sign of a developmental delay. Acting early can make a real difference .

Key times to be aware of:

At 12 months: If your child is not standing or attempting to walk, or isn't using gestures like pointing .

At 18 months: If your child is not walking or isn't saying at least a few words .

At 24 months: If your child is not combining two words into a phrase .

General concerns at any age:

Loss of skills they once had (regression).

Not meeting milestones in multiple areas.

No interest in interacting with others.

Extreme, unmanageable tantrums or difficulty with transitions.

What to Do If You Have a Concern

Talk to your pediatrician about your concerns and bring a completed milestone checklist .

Ask about developmental screening at your child's well visits .

Reach out for support:

For children under 3: Contact your state’s Early Intervention program for a free evaluation.

For children 3 and older: Contact your local public elementary school for an evaluation .

CDC's free Milestone Tracker App can help you track milestones and share summaries with your doctor .

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: When should I call the doctor about my child's development?

Call your pediatrician if your child is missing key milestones (like not walking by 18 months, or not using two-word phrases by 2 years), losing skills they once had, or if you have a persistent feeling something isn't right. You know your child best .

Q: How can I help my child develop new skills?

Play is the best way to help your child learn. Talk and read to them daily, encourage active play, and provide safe opportunities for exploration. Using CDC's Milestone Tracker app also offers tips on encouraging development .

Q: What happens if my child misses a milestone?

Missing a milestone doesn't automatically mean there's a problem, but it's a sign to be proactive. Talk to your pediatrician. They may recommend a formal developmental screening or a referral to a specialist to get your child the support they need .
