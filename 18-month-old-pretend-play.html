Pretend Play in Your 18-Month-Old: A Guide for Parents

Pretend play, also known as imaginative or fantasy play, is an important part of your child&#x27;s development. The normal development of children aged 1includes social skills like playing with others, taking turns, and doing fantasy play (Source: NIH). At 18 months, your child is beginning to engage in more complex forms of pretend play, which is a sign of healthy cognitive growth.

The Beginnings of Pretend Play

At 18 months, pretend play is becoming more sophisticated. You might see your child:

Using one object to represent another, such as pretending a block is a phone.

Acting out familiar routines, like feeding a doll or putting a toy to bed.

Engaging in simple role-play, like pretending to be a parent or a pet.

Imitating your actions, such as pretending to cook or clean.

Beginning to use language to support their play, such as saying &quot;night-night&quot; to a doll.

Why Pretend Play Matters

Pretend play is not just fun; it is essential for your child&#x27;s development. It helps them:

Develop language skills by using words to describe their actions.

Foster social and emotional skills, as they learn to understand different perspectives and practice empathy.

Build cognitive skills, such as problem-solving, creative thinking, and memory.

Process their experiences and make sense of the world around them.

How to Encourage Pretend Play

You can support your child&#x27;s imagination with simple activities:

Provide simple props: Offer dolls, stuffed animals, toy phones, empty boxes, and kitchen items.

Join the play: Get down on the floor and play with your child. Imitate their actions and add simple comments.

Read books: Reading stories introduces new ideas and characters, which can spark your child&#x27;s imagination.

Sing songs: Nursery rhymes and songs with actions are fun ways to encourage pretend play.

When to Talk to Your Pediatrician

Toddlers do not develop at the same rate (Source: NIH). However, if you are concerned that your child is not engaging in pretend play or is not reaching other developmental milestones, talk to your pediatrician. Your pediatrician can provide guidance and support.
